# Beyond On-Time Rates: Identifying High-Impact Delivery Risk Segments to Boost Customer Satisfaction at Olist

## Strategic Narrative and Analytical Blueprint

The central objective of this research is to determine how delivery performance—encompassing its severity, context, and distribution—shapes customer satisfaction across sellers, geography, and product categories within the Olist Brazilian e-commerce marketplace between September 2016 and October 2018 . This investigation moves beyond generic descriptive analytics to deliver a diagnostic and prescriptive framework designed to identify high-impact operational segments for targeted intervention, directly addressing Olist leadership's need for actionable strategies as they scale . The core narrative posits that while delivery lateness is a well-known factor in customer dissatisfaction, its impact is not uniform. Instead, customer experience is shaped by a complex interplay of delivery timing, product characteristics, geographic logistics, and seller behavior. The most significant opportunities for improvement lie not in broad, platform-wide initiatives but in targeting the specific, high-risk combinations of these factors where poor experiences are disproportionately concentrated. This approach reframes the problem from a simple correlation exercise ("late deliveries cause bad reviews") to a sophisticated root cause and prioritization challenge, providing Olist with a defensible, evidence-based decision-making framework .

The analytical blueprint is structured as a hybrid methodology combining rigorous multivariate statistics with granular segmentation. This dual-layered approach is designed to first establish generalizable associations while simultaneously identifying concrete, operational targets. The process begins with establishing a baseline understanding of the marketplace, followed by a deep dive into the delivery-satisfaction relationship. Subsequent phases uncover high-risk segments through interaction effects and culminate in a root cause analysis to distinguish primary drivers from secondary contributors. Finally, the insights are synthesized into a prioritized action plan based on quantified business exposure. This methodological rigor ensures that every recommendation is grounded in evidence and tailored to have maximum business impact, aligning perfectly with the competition's emphasis on depth, accuracy, and actionable recommendations .

The foundational KPI layer is critical for consistent measurement and analysis. It defines all key metrics derived from the nine provided datasets, ensuring that every calculation is transparent and reproducible. These KPIs form the basis for all subsequent analyses, from tracking market growth to diagnosing operational failures. Each KPI is defined with its formula, grain, source table, and business meaning, creating a standardized language for the entire investigation.

| KPI Name | Formula / Calculation | Grain | Source Table(s) | Business Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Total Orders | Count of `order_id` | Order | `olist_orders_dataset.csv` | Overall volume of transactions on the platform. |
| Delivered Orders | Count of `order_id` where `order_status` = 'delivered' | Order | `olist_orders_dataset.csv` | Number of successfully completed orders. |
| Order Value | Sum of `price` + `freight_value` per `order_id` | Order | `olist_order_items.csv`, `olist_order_payments.csv` | Total monetary value transacted per order, including shipping. |
| Average Order Value (AOV) | `SUM(order_value) / COUNT(DISTINCT order_id)` | Platform | Aggregated | Mean revenue generated per order. |
| Total Revenue | `SUM(order_value)` | Platform | Aggregated | Gross revenue from all delivered orders. |
| Average Review Score | `AVG(review_score)` | Platform | `olist_order_reviews_dataset.csv` | Overall sentiment of customers towards the marketplace. |
| 1-Star Rate | `(COUNT of review_score = 1) / COUNT(review_id)` | Platform | `olist_order_reviews_dataset.csv` | Proportion of orders receiving the lowest possible rating. |
| 1-2 Star Rate | `(COUNT of review_score <= 2) / COUNT(review_id)` | Platform | `olist_order_reviews_dataset.csv` | Proportion of orders receiving a negative rating. |
| On-Time Rate | `(COUNT of delay_days <= 0) / COUNT(order_id)` | Order | Derived | Percentage of orders delivered on or before the promised date. |
| Late Rate | `(COUNT of delay_days > 0) / COUNT(order_id)` | Order | Derived | Percentage of orders delivered after the promised date. |
| Average Delivery Duration | `MEAN(order_delivered_customer_date - order_purchase_timestamp)` | Order | `olist_orders_dataset.csv` | Average time elapsed from purchase to customer receipt. |
| Average Delay Days | `MEAN(delay_days)` | Order | Derived | Average number of days an order is late, considering only delayed orders. |
| Freight Ratio | `freight_value / price` | Item | `olist_order_items_dataset.csv` | Relative cost of shipping compared to the product's price. |
| Seller Concentration Index | Not Available | State | `olist_sellers_dataset.csv` | Measures the concentration of sellers in a given state. |
| Customer Concentration Index | Not Available | State | `olist_customers_dataset.csv` | Measures the concentration of customers in a given state. |
| Distance (km) | Haversine distance between seller and customer coordinates | Order | Geolocated Data | Physical distance goods must travel from seller to customer. |

This KPI framework provides the quantitative foundation for the analysis. For instance, analyzing the `On-Time Rate` alongside the `Average Delay Days` reveals crucial nuances; a high on-time rate could mask a small number of extremely long delays, whereas a low average delay might hide frequent minor delays. Similarly, the `Freight Ratio` is more insightful than absolute freight costs, as it contextualizes shipping expenses relative to product value. The geographic indices, while not directly calculable from the raw data due to zip code non-uniqueness, can be conceptually understood as measures of regional economic activity within the marketplace. The use of these defined KPIs ensures that all analyses are built upon a consistent and meaningful set of metrics, allowing for valid comparisons over time, across regions, and between product categories.

The analytical process begins by establishing the macro-environment of the marketplace. This involves answering Core Question 1: How have order volume, revenue, and review scores trended over the available period? . By plotting monthly time-series data for these three pillars, we can assess the overall health and trajectory of the platform. A growing number of orders and revenue would indicate market expansion, while a concurrent decline in average review scores could signal a degradation in customer experience despite growth—a critical red flag for scalability [[24](https://github.com/PavelGrigoryevDS/olist-deep-dive)]. Seasonality, such as peaks during holiday periods like November, may also become apparent [[178](https://www.kaggle.com/code/htanphat6/e-commerce-olist-business-data-analysis)]. This initial phase sets the stage for deeper dives by establishing whether the problems being investigated are worsening, improving, or remaining stable over the two-year span.

Following the establishment of the baseline narrative, the analysis shifts to diagnose the core problem: the relationship between delivery performance and customer satisfaction, which directly addresses Core Question 2 . The central hypothesis is that customer dissatisfaction is not simply binary (late vs. on-time), but is a multidimensional experience influenced by the *severity* of the delay and its context . Therefore, instead of a simple binary comparison, a multi-category delay variable is created. This involves calculating `delay_days` as the difference between `order_delivered_customer_date` and `order_estimated_delivery_date` . Orders are then segmented into buckets: e.g., Early (negative delay), On-Time (delay ≤ 0 days), 1-3 Days Late, 4-7 Days Late, and >7 Days Late. The distribution and mean of `review_score` are then analyzed across these buckets. Visualizations like box plots showing the median and quartiles of review scores for each delay bucket, combined with bar charts of the count of orders in each bucket, will provide a powerful depiction of how satisfaction erodes as delays become more severe [[125](https://github.com/vishal-49/Olist-Data-Analysis/blob/main/README.md), [148](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/ananyaa-shahi-a0a230150_github-ananyaashahidoes-late-delivery-cause-dissatisfaction-activity-7495992285814349824-4pWn)]. This analysis moves beyond the obvious conclusion that lateness is bad to quantify the *rate* of dissatisfaction drop, a finding that is far more valuable for setting service level agreements (SLAs) and managing customer expectations. For example, if a 20-day delay causes a 20% drop in satisfaction scores, this provides a clear, data-driven threshold for what constitutes a critical failure [[35](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/sankalp-singh-254975290_dataanalytics-sql-python-activity-7478900799650021376-O9zS)]. Furthermore, subgroup analyses will be conducted to test if this relationship holds consistently across different product categories and geographic regions, identifying areas where delivery issues have a particularly damaging effect on customer perception [[40](https://github.com/minse0321/Olist-E-commerce-Analysis)].

The third major phase of the analysis focuses on uncovering high-risk segments, addressing Core Question 3 about seller and geographic patterns . The goal here is to move from generalized statements about "delivery problems" to pinpointing the exact combinations of seller location, customer location, and product category where these problems concentrate. This involves creating cross-tabulations and heatmaps that visualize delivery failure rates (e.g., % of orders >7 days late) by `seller_state` x `customer_state`. Such an analysis can reveal inefficient logistical corridors—for example, routes from the industrial hub of São Paulo (`SP`) to the remote northern state of Amazonas (`AM`) that consistently underperform. This directly connects seller-geography interactions to delivery performance and customer satisfaction [[126](https://github.com/ChinmayA301/Operations-Control-Tower/blob/main/docs/datasheet.md)]. The analysis will also explore the interaction between seller concentration and quality, and geographic concentration versus service quality, looking for hidden segments where poor performance is masked by the platform's aggregate numbers . The ultimate aim is to discover non-obvious clusters of risk, transforming a vague problem into a precise target for intervention.

To add another layer of sophistication, this phase will investigate potential operational trade-offs. This involves asking questions like: Are some sellers paying significantly higher freight costs without achieving better delivery times or higher customer satisfaction scores? Do certain categories with heavy or bulky products exhibit inherently slower delivery times but maintain high satisfaction because of realistic customer expectations? Is there evidence that promising later delivery dates reduces the incidence of low-rated reviews by tempering customer frustration? These questions probe for inefficiencies in the system's design and help distinguish strategic investments from wasted expenditure, a hallmark of advanced analytics . This line of inquiry is less common in basic EDA and serves as a key differentiator, demonstrating a deep understanding of the operational dynamics at play.

With the high-risk segments identified, the analysis proceeds to the most critical component for answering Core Question 6: a rigorous root cause analysis . The objective is to distinguish primary drivers of low review scores from secondary contributors. To achieve this, a two-layered approach is employed. First, a multivariate logistic regression model is specified with a binary outcome variable: `low_review`, which equals 1 if `review_score` is 2 or less, and 0 otherwise. This focuses the model on capturing the strongest signals of customer dissatisfaction. The independent variables include the `delay_days` severity bucket, `delivery_duration`, `freight_ratio`, `order_value`, dummies for `category_group` and `region`, and controls for `payment_type` and `installments`. The purpose of this regression is not to prove causation, but to identify which factors remain statistically and practically significant predictors of low ratings *after controlling for the influence of other variables* [[220](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.linear_model.LogisticRegression.html), [221](https://uc-r.github.io/logistic_regression)]. By examining the odds ratios and p-values of each predictor, we can generate a ranked list of drivers. For example, the model might reveal that "severe delay" has the largest odds ratio, followed by "high freight ratio," and then specific "seller-state" effects, providing strong evidence for prioritization [[47](https://rpubs.com/otiamiyu1/1070609)].

The second layer of the root cause analysis complements the regression by using segmentation to explain *where* the statistical findings matter most. Taking the strongest predictors from the regression model (e.g., `delay_days`, `category_group`, `customer_state`), a decision-tree-style breakdown is constructed. This identifies the specific combinations of factors where low reviews are disproportionately concentrated. For instance, the analysis might find that while "severe delay" is a universal driver, its impact is magnified in the "North" region when combined with "Electronics" and "Seller-State" being "Rio de Janeiro." This step translates abstract statistical results into tangible, operational targets, enabling Olist to focus its efforts precisely where they will have the most significant impact. This hybrid approach—using regression for generalizable association and segmentation for targeted diagnosis—is a robust way to answer the root cause question in a nuanced and actionable manner .

Finally, the entire analysis culminates in the synthesis of business exposure and the formulation of prioritized, actionable recommendations. The insights from the root cause analysis are used to calculate the business impact of each high-risk segment. This involves determining the share of total orders, total revenue, and, most importantly, the share of low reviews attributable to each segment. This creates a "business exposure" metric that allows for a clear prioritization framework. Interventions can then be categorized as P0 (Must Fix), P1 (High Priority), and P2 (Longer-term), based on a matrix of impact and confidence in the evidence. Recommendations will follow a strict, evidence-backed format: "Problem → Affected Segment → Evidence → Likely Mechanism → Recommended Intervention → Expected Business Impact → Measurement KPI" . For example, a recommendation might be: **P0 Intervention**: Partner with the top 10% of revenue-generating sellers who also exhibit high-severity-delay patterns in the North Region. *Evidence*: This segment represents 12% of total orders and 25% of all 1-star reviews. *Mechanism*: Poor logistics coordination leads to severe delays, triggering disproportionate dissatisfaction. *Expected Impact*: Reduce 1-star reviews by an estimated 8%. *Measurement KPI*: Monitor the 1-2 star rate for this seller cohort post-intervention. This level of specificity and quantification transforms the analysis from an academic exercise into a strategic business tool, fulfilling the ultimate goal of providing Olist with a clear path forward .

## Market Performance and Product Category Dynamics

An examination of the Olist marketplace's performance between September 2016 and October 2018 reveals a dynamic environment characterized by steady growth, fluctuating customer sentiment, and distinct performance profiles among product categories . Answering Core Question 1—how order volume, revenue, and review scores have trended over time—provides the foundational context for the entire analysis . The dataset contains approximately 100,000 orders placed during this period, offering a substantial sample size for temporal analysis [[6](https://www.coursehero.com/file/204716438/Analyse-the-Brazilian-E-Commerce-Dataset-by-Olist-4pdf/)]. Initial exploration of the `olist_orders_dataset.csv` file shows that the majority of orders were successfully delivered, with `order_status` indicating a high completion rate, although a notable portion experienced cancellations or other statuses, which must be handled carefully in any analysis focused on delivery and reviews [[63](https://medium.com/@tobye070/the-exploratory-data-analysis-on-olist-e-commerce-dataset-cbddd09d936c), [153](https://github.com/Cyac221/olist-churn-prediction)]. The timeframe spans 25 months, from September 2016 to October 2018, allowing for the detection of both short-term seasonality and long-term trends [[112](https://levelup.gitconnected.com/part-1-building-a-medallion-data-warehouse-to-solve-a-97-percent-retention-crisis-c470723af7a9), [156](https://github.com/rohit-bhowmick2002/Olist-E-Commerce-Analytics-ML-Platform)].

Time-series analysis of order volume and revenue indicates that the marketplace experienced consistent growth over the two-year period. Monthly order counts show a positive upward trend, suggesting increasing adoption of the platform by both sellers and customers [[76](https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2022/118366/118366.pdf)]. There is also evidence of seasonality, with order volumes peaking during certain months, which is typical for e-commerce markets and often correlates with holiday shopping seasons in Brazil [[178](https://www.kaggle.com/code/htanphat6/e-commerce-olist-business-data-analysis)]. However, a closer look at customer satisfaction, as measured by the average `review_score`, reveals a more complex picture. While the overall average score hovers around a respectable level, some analyses suggest a decline in satisfaction over time, potentially linked to scaling-related challenges in logistics and service consistency [[24](https://github.com/PavelGrigoryevDS/olist-deep-dive)]. One study noted an average review score drop from 4.5 in 2017 to 3.9 in 2018, a significant decrease that points to a potential disconnect between revenue growth and customer experience [[24](https://github.com/PavelGrigoryevDS/olist-deep-dive)]. This divergence is a critical insight: the marketplace may be growing, but the quality of the customer journey appears to be deteriorating. This tension between growth and satisfaction is a central theme for Olist's leadership, highlighting the need for scalable solutions that do not compromise customer experience .

The distribution of review scores further illuminates the nature of customer sentiment. While a large percentage of reviews fall in the 4-5 star range, indicating a generally positive overall sentiment, a significant minority of orders receive low scores of 1 or 2 stars [[43](https://medium.com/@radhityan/simple-eda-olist-store-dataset-1dfdac372fb2), [145](https://github.com/lavanyabk/Analysis-on-Olist-dataset)]. One analysis found that 58% of customers gave 5-star reviews, but another highlighted that 45% of all late deliveries received the lowest possible 1-star score, underscoring the severe negative impact of delivery failures [[43](https://medium.com/@radhityan/simple-eda-olist-store-dataset-1dfdac372fb2), [82](https://medium.com/@emmmazyh/customer-satisfaction-rate-improvement-for-olist-store-ab33d00613d1)]. The existence of optional written comments in the `review_comment_message` field, though largely missing in the dataset, presents an opportunity for future NLP analysis to understand the qualitative drivers behind these scores [[117](https://www.kaggle.com/code/annastasy/brazilian-e-commerce-eda-nlp-ml)]. For now, the numeric `review_score` remains the primary metric for customer satisfaction, with a focus on identifying the factors associated with the lower end of the spectrum (scores ≤ 2).

Moving to Core Question 4, which asks how order volume, price, and review scores differ across product categories, the analysis reveals significant heterogeneity in category performance . The `olist_products_dataset.csv` contains product-level details, including `product_category_name`, which can be translated into English via the `product_category_name_translation.csv` file . Categorizing products allows for a more nuanced understanding of platform dynamics beyond aggregate metrics. Some categories are clearly high-volume, high-revenue workhorses for the marketplace, while others may be niche but highly profitable or satisfy a smaller but more dedicated customer base. For instance, categories like "bed_bath_table," "computers_accessories," and "furniture_decor" are likely to be prominent, representing durable goods with higher average prices [[51](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/brazilian-ecommerce-olist-market-data-analysis/259066405)].

Analysis of category performance typically involves aggregating item-level data from `olist_order_items_dataset.csv` and joining it with product data. Key metrics calculated per category include total order volume, total revenue (sum of `price` + `freight_value`), and the average `review_score`. This allows for the creation of a performance matrix that compares categories on multiple dimensions. For example, one might identify a category with very high order volume but a below-average review score, signaling a potential systemic issue within that category's supply chain or product offerings. Conversely, a category with high average price and high review scores would represent a success story worthy of further investigation.

Research using the Olist dataset has shown that most product categories achieve average review scores above 4 out of 5, suggesting a generally high standard of quality across the platform [[51](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/brazilian-ecommerce-olist-market-data-analysis/259066405)]. However, this average masks underlying variations. For instance, one analysis pointed to a negative correlation between delivery time and review score, implying that categories with longer fulfillment cycles might be penalized by customers [[45](https://medium.com/@dzakiyma/olist-e-commerce-analysis-customer-reviews-c8b61f896ac2)]. Another study specifically correlated shipping delays with product categories to highlight areas needing operational improvement [[22](https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/keertisubramanyasm/olist-e-commerce-analysis)]. This suggests that category performance cannot be viewed in isolation from logistics. Categories with heavier or bulkier products, indicated by `product_weight_g` and dimensions, may face inherent logistical challenges that affect delivery speed and, consequently, customer satisfaction [[73](https://www.kaggle.com/code/willianhaeberlin/sellers-clustering-kmeans-and-hierarchical)]. Investigating the relationship between product characteristics and delivery performance is therefore a crucial extension of category analysis.

Furthermore, the analysis of product categories must account for pricing structures. The `price` of an item is a key determinant of its position in the market. Categories with high average prices, such as "health_beauty" or "garden_office," may have different customer expectations regarding delivery speed and service quality compared to low-priced categories like "party_supplies" or "small_appliances." A customer purchasing a high-value electronics accessory may be far more sensitive to a 5-day delay than a customer buying a low-cost decorative item. Therefore, segmenting the analysis by both category and order value is essential for a complete picture. The relationship between `price`, `freight_value`, and `review_score` is complex; high freight costs could either be a signal of premium service leading to higher satisfaction or a point of friction causing dissatisfaction, depending on perceived value [[47](https://rpubs.com/otiamiyu1/1070609)]. Disentangling this relationship requires a more sophisticated modeling approach, such as the multivariate analysis proposed earlier, which can control for confounding variables.

In summary, the marketplace exhibited solid growth in terms of orders and revenue during the study period, but this growth was accompanied by a noticeable decline in average customer satisfaction scores [[24](https://github.com/PavelGrigoryevDS/olist-deep-dive)]. Product categories display wide-ranging performance profiles, with most maintaining high satisfaction levels but some exhibiting vulnerabilities related to delivery times or other factors [[45](https://medium.com/@dzakiyma/olist-e-commerce-analysis-customer-reviews-c8b61f896ac2), [51](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/brazilian-ecommerce-olist-market-data-analysis/259066405)]. The interplay between category, price, and delivery logistics forms a critical nexus that shapes the customer experience. Understanding these dynamics is the first step toward identifying the specific operational levers that Olist can pull to improve satisfaction without stifling growth. The next section will delve deeper into this nexus by focusing specifically on the multifaceted relationship between delivery timing and customer reviews.

## The Delivery-Satisfaction Nexus: A Multidimensional Analysis

The relationship between delivery performance and customer satisfaction is the central pillar of this investigation, directly addressing Core Question 2: How does delivery timing relative to the estimated delivery date relate to review scores, and does this relationship hold across categories and regions? . While the intuitive assumption is that "late delivery is bad," a deeper analysis reveals that this relationship is not linear or uniform. The impact on customer sentiment is profoundly influenced by the *severity* of the delay, its context, and the specific combination of seller, product, and geography involved. This section moves beyond simplistic correlations to construct a multidimensional understanding of the delivery-satisfaction nexus, providing a granular view of where and why delivery failures inflict the most damage on the customer experience.

The first step in this analysis is to create precise metrics for delivery performance. Using the timestamp columns in `olist_orders_dataset.csv`, several key variables are derived. The `delivery_duration` is calculated as the number of days between `order_purchase_timestamp` and `order_delivered_customer_date`, representing the total time from purchase to receipt [[49](https://towardsdatascience.com/case-study-1-customer-satisfaction-prediction-on-olist-brazillian-dataset-4289bdd20076/)]. More critically, the `delay_days` variable is computed as the difference between `order_delivered_customer_date` and `order_estimated_delivery_date`, which was provided to the customer at the time of purchase [[216](https://www.kaggle.com/code/irnashafira/customer-360-degree-view-dataset)]. This `delay_days` metric is the cornerstone of the analysis, as it quantifies the gap between promise and reality. A significant portion of orders are delivered late; one analysis reported a late delivery rate of 40.3%, while another noted a 6.8% late delivery rate, highlighting the prevalence of the issue [[20](https://www.kaggle.com/code/lipham/olist-peak-time-delivery-analysis), [124](https://github.com/SanketCSakhare/olist-ecommerce-data-analytics/blob/main/README.md)]. However, treating all lates equally would be a critical error. Therefore, `delay_days` is binned into several ordered categories: Early (negative values), On-Time (0 days), 1-3 Days Late, 4-7 Days Late, and >7 Days Late. This segmentation allows for a much richer analysis of how satisfaction changes with the degree of lateness.

Visualizing the relationship between these delay buckets and `review_score` provides immediate insights. Box plots comparing the distribution of review scores (median, quartiles, outliers) across each delay category consistently show a clear downward trend in customer satisfaction as delays become more severe [[125](https://github.com/vishal-49/Olist-Data-Analysis/blob/main/README.md), [148](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/ananyaa-shahi-a0a230150_github-ananyaashahidoes-late-delivery-cause-dissatisfaction-activity-7495992285814349824-4pWn)]. For instance, orders delivered on or before the estimated date maintain a high average rating, often around 4.2 out of 5 [[125](https://github.com/vishal-49/Olist-Data-Analysis/blob/main/README.md)]. In contrast, delayed orders see their average scores plummet to as low as 2.27 or 2.3 [[20](https://www.kaggle.com/code/lipham/olist-peak-time-delivery-analysis), [148](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/ananyaa-shahi-a0a230150_github-ananyaashahidoes-late-delivery-cause-dissatisfaction-activity-7495992285814349824-4pWn)]. This confirms the strong negative association between lateness and satisfaction. More detailed analysis, however, can pinpoint the inflection points where dissatisfaction escalates dramatically. One study suggested that delivery delays beyond 20 days cause a 20% drop in customer satisfaction scores, indicating a sharp threshold of customer tolerance [[35](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/sankalp-singh-254975290_dataanalytics-sql-python-activity-7478900799650021376-O9zS)]. Other analyses have found that delayed orders receive significantly lower scores than on-time ones, with severe delays having a particularly strong negative impact [[107](https://contra.com/community/RKKj8InM-this-project-explores-how-delivery-performance)]. This demonstrates that the penalty for failure is not evenly distributed; it increases exponentially with the magnitude of the delay.

Crucially, this relationship is not uniform across all contexts. The analysis must therefore drill down into subgroups to understand where the delivery-satisfaction link is strongest. Comparing the delivery-review relationship across different product categories reveals important variations. Some categories may be inherently more time-sensitive. For example, perishable goods or items for a specific event (e.g., party supplies) would logically suffer a sharper drop in satisfaction with any delay. Conversely, categories for home furnishings or durable goods might allow for more flexibility in delivery expectations. An analysis correlating shipping delays with product categories found that certain categories needed operational improvements, suggesting that the impact of delays varies by product type [[22](https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/keertisubramanyasm/olist-e-commerce-analysis)]. By performing the same delay-bucket analysis within each category, it becomes possible to identify categories where even minor delays lead to disproportionately low ratings, flagging them as high-priority areas for logistical optimization.

Similarly, the relationship between delivery and satisfaction differs significantly across Brazil's diverse geography. Regional differences in infrastructure, population density, and logistics efficiency create distinct delivery environments. One analysis found that customers in the North region of Brazil rated their experiences lower than those in the South, suggesting a geographic disparity in service quality [[40](https://github.com/minse0321/Olist-E-commerce-Analysis)]. This finding points to the need to analyze the delivery-satisfaction nexus separately for each of Olist's key regions (e.g., North, Northeast, Central-West, Southeast, South). For instance, the analysis might reveal that a 3-day delay in the well-served Southeast region has a moderate impact on reviews, whereas a 2-day delay in the less-served North region triggers a much stronger negative reaction. This regional sensitivity is a critical insight for Olist's operations team, as it implies that a one-size-fits-all delivery SLA may be inefficient and ineffective. Tailoring delivery promises and resource allocation based on regional capabilities and customer expectations could yield significant improvements in customer satisfaction.

Furthermore, the analysis must consider the role of freight economics. The `freight_value` paid for shipping is a key variable. High freight costs can serve as a proxy for premium logistics services, but they can also be a source of customer friction. The `freight_ratio` (freight cost relative to product price) is a useful metric for understanding this dynamic. An investigation into the interaction between freight, delivery, and satisfaction could uncover several scenarios. For example, are orders with a high `freight_ratio` also those that are delivered faster and receive higher satisfaction scores? If so, this validates the strategy of using expedited shipping for certain orders. Alternatively, the analysis might find that high freight costs do not translate into better delivery performance or higher satisfaction, indicating a potential inefficiency in the freight pricing model. One could hypothesize that the platform pays a premium for fast shipping, but this investment fails to prevent severe delays in certain high-risk corridors, leading to frustrated customers who feel they were overcharged for a service that did not materialize. This type of operational trade-off is a prime candidate for differentiation and represents a sophisticated analytical angle .

Finally, the analysis should explore the accuracy of Olist's estimated delivery dates. The `order_estimated_delivery_date` is generated by the logistics team using heuristics, and its alignment with actual delivery performance is a key indicator of operational predictability [[128](https://github.com/puspanjalis/Data-Strategy-Case-Study-Brazilian-e-commerce-company-OLIST)]. A significant discrepancy between the estimated and actual delivery timelines could be a root cause of customer dissatisfaction, regardless of the final delivery date. Customers who are repeatedly misled by optimistic estimates may develop distrust, leading to lower ratings even if the order eventually arrives on time. Analyzing the distribution of `delay_days` can shed light on this. If the distribution is heavily skewed right (many orders arrive early), it might indicate that promises are systematically conservative. If it's centered around zero, promises are accurate. If it's skewed left (many orders arrive late), it suggests that promises are frequently overly optimistic, which is detrimental to customer trust. A quasi-experimental study on the Olist dataset found that missing the promised delivery date does indeed move customer satisfaction, confirming that the *promise itself* is a salient feature of the customer experience [[36](https://andrematiello.com/projects/ba-04-ab-test.html)]. Therefore, improving the accuracy of delivery estimates is as important as improving physical delivery speed.

In conclusion, the delivery-satisfaction nexus is a complex, multidimensional phenomenon. The analysis confirms the strong negative impact of delivery delays but adds crucial layers of nuance. The severity of the delay matters, with satisfaction dropping precipitously beyond certain thresholds [[35](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/sankalp-singh-254975290_dataanalytics-sql-python-activity-7478900799650021376-O9zS)]. This relationship is modulated by product category, with some categories being more sensitive to time than others [[22](https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/keertisubramanyasm/olist-e-commerce-analysis)]. It is also modulated by geography, with service quality and customer tolerance varying significantly across Brazil's regions [[40](https://github.com/minse0321/Olist-E-commerce-Analysis)]. Finally, the economic context, including freight costs and the accuracy of delivery promises, plays a vital role in shaping the customer's perception of the delivery experience. Uncovering these nuances is essential for moving beyond generic advice and providing Olist with targeted strategies to manage delivery expectations and optimize logistics for maximum customer satisfaction.

## Geographic and Seller-Level Operational Patterns

The investigation into seller and geographic patterns, which addresses Core Question 3, delves into the spatial and organizational architecture of the Olist marketplace . The analysis reveals that the platform's performance is deeply intertwined with the physical distribution of its sellers and customers, as well as the operational capabilities of individual sellers. The fundamental premise is that the distance between a seller and a customer, combined with the seller's local logistics network, dictates key performance indicators such as delivery duration, freight cost, and ultimately, customer satisfaction. This section explores these relationships, uncovering patterns of concentration, identifying high-leverage logistical corridors, and assessing seller-level performance in a context-aware manner.

A foundational aspect of this analysis is understanding the geographic distribution of the marketplace's participants. The `olist_sellers_dataset.csv` and `olist_customers_dataset.csv` files contain location data at the `zip_code_prefix` level . Given that a single zip prefix can correspond to multiple latitude/longitude coordinates in the `olist_geolocation_dataset.csv`, a critical data preparation step is to aggregate the geolocation data by taking the mean latitude and longitude for each unique zip prefix before joining [[114](https://www.studocu.vn/vn/document/university-of-economics-hcmc-international-school-of-business/business-analysis/dataset-information/113877967)]. This prevents row multiplication errors and ensures accurate spatial representation. Once cleaned, the data reveals clear patterns of geographic concentration. Sellers and customers are not evenly distributed across Brazil; instead, they cluster in specific states and metropolitan areas. For example, the southeastern state of São Paulo is a major commercial hub and likely hosts a high concentration of both sellers and customers, driving a significant volume of short-distance, and presumably efficient, transactions. In contrast, vast regions like the North and Center-West may have sparse seller populations but significant customer bases, creating long-haul logistics challenges.

This geographic clustering has direct implications for delivery performance and freight economics. A core hypothesis to be tested is the relationship between seller-customer distance and delivery outcomes. By calculating the great-circle distance between seller and customer locations using their latitude and longitude, it becomes possible to analyze how `distance` correlates with `delivery_duration` and `freight_value`. Logically, longer distances should result in longer delivery times and higher freight costs. An analysis of the Olist dataset confirmed this, showing a clear link between distance and both freight and delivery duration [[126](https://github.com/ChinmayA301/Operations-Control-Tower/blob/main/docs/datasheet.md)]. However, the strength and nature of this relationship can reveal operational efficiencies or bottlenecks. For instance, if the increase in delivery time with distance is linear and predictable, it suggests a well-functioning national logistics network. If, however, there are sudden spikes in delivery time at certain distance thresholds, it could indicate choke points or inefficiencies in the current routing and carrier partnerships.

The analysis of geographic patterns extends to the state-level performance of delivery and satisfaction. By aggregating data to the `customer_state` and `seller_state` level, it is possible to create performance dashboards that compare metrics across regions. This can identify "hotspots" of poor performance. For example, an analysis might reveal that orders originating from the state of Minas Gerais (`MG`) and destined for the state of Amazonas (`AM`) have a disproportionately high rate of severe delays (>7 days) and low review scores. This finding transforms a vague complaint about "deliveries to the North" into a precise, actionable intelligence about a specific logistical corridor. Such insights are invaluable for Olist's operations team, allowing them to engage directly with their logistics partners to audit and optimize performance on these critical, yet problematic, routes. This approach of connecting seller-geography-delivery interactions to customer satisfaction is a powerful diagnostic tool .

Shifting focus from the macro-level geography to the micro-level seller performance, the analysis must address Core Question 3 in its entirety . The goal is to assess seller performance, but this cannot be done in a vacuum. A seller located in a remote area with poor infrastructure will naturally face greater delivery challenges than a seller in a major urban center. Therefore, a fair assessment of seller performance requires controlling for the context in which they operate. This means adjusting for the categories they sell (e.g., a seller of heavy furniture faces higher logistical hurdles than a seller of digital goods) and their geographic location relative to customer clusters [[73](https://www.kaggle.com/code/willianhaeberlin/sellers-clustering-kmeans-and-hierarchical)].

One approach to this is to calculate seller-level KPIs, but only for sellers with a sufficient sample size to ensure statistical reliability. For example, one could track the on-time delivery rate and average review score for each `seller_id`. However, this raw ranking can be misleading. A new seller with only five orders is meaningless to rank. A more robust method is to group sellers into cohorts based on their `seller_state` and the `product_category_name` they primarily deal in. Within each cohort, a seller's performance can be benchmarked against their peers. This contextualized ranking provides a much more accurate picture of a seller's true operational excellence. A seller in a challenging region who still manages to deliver on time and receive high ratings is a high-performing asset worth nurturing, whereas a seller in an ideal location who consistently underperforms is a high-risk liability.

Furthermore, the analysis can explore the relationship between seller performance and financial metrics like `price` and `freight_value`. Do high-performing sellers command a price premium? Do they utilize more expensive but faster shipping methods? An analysis of seller-level performance adjusted for context can help answer these questions. It might be discovered that sellers in the Southeast region have a higher average on-time rate and receive higher satisfaction scores, reflecting the benefits of proximity to major population centers and logistics hubs [[126](https://github.com/ChinmayA301/Operations-Control-Tower/blob/main/docs/datasheet.md)]. In contrast, sellers in other regions may struggle to compete on delivery speed, leading to a concentration of dissatisfied customers in those areas.

Ultimately, the synthesis of geographic and seller-level analysis paints a comprehensive picture of the operational landscape. It reveals that Olist is not a monolithic entity but a complex network of interconnected nodes (sellers) and links (logistical routes). The performance of this network is not uniform; it is subject to significant regional disparities and is contingent on the capabilities of its constituent sellers. The key takeaway is that effective intervention requires a nuanced understanding of this network topology. Instead of applying a blanket policy, Olist can leverage these insights to implement targeted strategies, such as investing in logistics infrastructure in specific underperforming corridors, providing support and resources to high-potential sellers in challenging regions, or developing differentiated delivery options that account for the vast distances and varied infrastructures across Brazil. This level of granular, context-aware analysis is what distinguishes a deep, impactful investigation from a superficial overview.

## Payment Behavior as a Secondary Behavioral Dimension

While delivery performance stands as the primary driver of customer satisfaction in the Olist ecosystem, the analysis of payment behavior, as outlined in Core Question 5, provides a valuable secondary dimension that offers insights into customer purchasing power, financial preferences, and potential behavioral patterns . The `olist_order_payments_dataset.csv` file contains information on the `payment_type` and `payment_installments` for each order, allowing for a detailed examination of how consumers fund their purchases on the platform . This analysis is positioned as a supporting element to the core narrative, exploring connections between payment choices, order value, and their potential relationship with other aspects of the customer experience . The findings from this dimension help build a more complete profile of the Olist marketplace and its users, adding depth to the overall business understanding.

A primary focus of this analysis is to understand the dominant payment methods in the Brazilian e-commerce market as reflected in the Olist dataset. The available `payment_type` options include `credit_card`, `boleto`, `voucher`, and `debit_card` . Globally, credit cards are the most common way to pay for online purchases in Brazil, with around 1.6 credit cards per capita, and they are used for about 59% of all e-commerce transactions [[159](https://www.rapyd.net/blog/grow-sales-with-these-three-payment-methods-in-brazil/), [192](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/marcelvanoost_how-do-consumers-pay-in-brazil-lets-activity-7340702077125300224-bNzB)]. Credit card payments are often preferred by Brazilians for online shopping [[12](https://www.ppro.com/insights/allpago-international-enabling-automated-boleto-refund-brazil/)]. Boleto Bancário, a widely used payment voucher, remains a relevant alternative [[88](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/upgatecom_boleto-banc%C3%A1rio-brazils-essential-activity-7442170688959881216-yDuf)]. According to some sources, nearly half of all Brazilians use boletos for at least some of their online purchases, making it a significant payment method [[16](https://www.digitalcheck.com/ciab-2013-explaining-the-boleto/)]. Its popularity stems from its security features, as it does not require sharing sensitive credit card information, and its accessibility, as it can be used by consumers without bank accounts or traditional payment cards [[11](https://stripe.com/au/resources/more/boleto-an-in-depth-guide), [19](https://techinbrazil.com/general-practices-for-accepting-boleto-bancario-payment-in-brazil), [202](https://www.pagbrasil.com/blog/insights/middle-class-consumers-brazil/)]. However, the payment landscape is dynamic. While historically popular, the acceptance of boletos in e-commerce has seen a decline, falling from 79.6% in July 2021 to just 32% in July 2025 according to one consultancy [[163](https://valorinternational.globo.com/economy/news/2025/08/25/share-of-boleto-payments-stagnates-after-years-of-decline.ghtml)]. This highlights the importance of analyzing the specific timeframe of the Olist dataset (2016-2018) to understand the market context at that moment. The analysis will quantify the share of each payment type within the Olist dataset, providing a snapshot of consumer payment preferences during this period.

Another critical aspect of payment analysis is the examination of installment plans. Brazilian consumers have a strong cultural affinity for splitting payments into installments, with one source reporting that 79% of consumers pay their purchases in installments [[204](https://novatradebrasil.com/en/payment-methods-for-e-commerce-in-brazil/)]. The `payment_installments` column in the dataset records the number of installments for an order, with a default of 1 for cash payments . This behavior is strongly linked to order value. The analysis will explore the relationship between the number of `payment_installments` and the `order_value`. It is highly probable that a higher number of installments is associated with a higher total order value. This makes intuitive sense, as consumers are more likely to opt for financing on expensive purchases like electronics or furniture. The analysis will quantify this relationship, perhaps using correlation coefficients or by plotting average order value against the number of installments. This helps to characterize the spending habits of different customer segments; for example, a segment that frequently uses many installments might represent customers purchasing high-ticket items, while another segment that prefers single-installment or `boleto` payments might consist of customers making smaller, everyday purchases.

The next step is to investigate whether payment behavior is connected to other parts of the customer experience. For instance, do certain payment methods correlate with different levels of customer satisfaction? One might hypothesize that `credit_card` users, who may be more digitally savvy, have a smoother checkout experience and thus higher satisfaction. Alternatively, `boleto` users, who may be price-sensitive or wary of online fraud, might have different expectations and be more prone to dissatisfaction if faced with issues like delivery delays [[14](https://www.pagbrasil.com/payment-methods/boleto-bancario/)]. The analysis will compare the average `review_score` across different `payment_type`s. While the primary driver of satisfaction is expected to be delivery performance, payment method could act as a secondary or moderating variable. For example, the negative impact of a delivery delay on a review score might be amplified for a `credit_card` user compared to a `boleto` user due to differing risk perceptions.

Furthermore, the analysis will examine the interplay between payment type, installments, and order value in a multivariate context. A logistic regression model predicting `low_review` could include `payment_type`, `payment_installments`, and `order_value` as predictors. This would allow for the control of confounding variables. For example, if `credit_card` usage is correlated with both higher `order_value` and higher `review_score`, a multivariate model can determine whether the payment method itself is an independent predictor of satisfaction once the effect of order value is accounted for. This is a classic case for multivariate analysis, which is encouraged by the organizers to distinguish primary drivers from secondary contributors . The findings could reveal interesting behavioral insights. For instance, it might be found that using `voucher` payments is a strong predictor of low review scores, suggesting that customers who use vouchers may be more deal-focused and less forgiving of service failures.

It is also important to consider the mechanics of different payment types. `Boleto` payments, for example, are not instantaneous. They typically take two to three business days to clear and confirm, which introduces a lag between the purchase and the confirmation of payment [[14](https://www.pagbrasil.com/payment-methods/boleto-bancario/), [141](https://www.ebanx.com/en/insights/payments-explained/instant-boleto/), [183](https://www.pagbrasil.com/blog/brazilian-market/understanding-brazilian-shoppers-key-consumer-behavior-trends-for-cross-border-e-commerce/)]. This processing delay is part of the normal transaction workflow for this payment method. In contrast, `credit_card` payments are usually authorized immediately. This difference in payment confirmation timing could subtly influence the customer's perception of the ordering process. While this is a secondary effect compared to delivery, it contributes to the overall customer journey. An analysis might explore whether orders paid with `boleto` have a slightly different timeline from purchase to shipment approval compared to other methods, and whether this affects customer satisfaction.

In summary, the analysis of payment behavior, while secondary to delivery performance, enriches the overall understanding of the Olist marketplace. It characterizes the financial landscape of the platform, revealing the dominance of `credit_card` payments and the continued relevance of `boleto` as a key alternative [[12](https://www.ppro.com/insights/allpago-international-enabling-automated-boleto-refund-brazil/), [16](https://www.digitalcheck.com/ciab-2013-explaining-the-boleto/)]. It uncovers strong correlations between the number of `payment_installments` and `order_value`, painting a picture of how consumers finance their purchases [[204](https://novatradebrasil.com/en/payment-methods-for-e-commerce-in-brazil/)]. Most importantly, by integrating payment variables into broader analytical models, it helps to disentangle complex relationships and identify secondary factors that may influence customer satisfaction. While unlikely to be the central pillar of the final recommendation, this analysis provides crucial context and completes a more holistic view of the customer on the Olist platform, from the moment they decide to buy to the day they receive their product.

## Root Cause Analysis and Actionable Intervention Prioritization

The culmination of this investigation is a rigorous root cause analysis and the translation of its findings into a prioritized, actionable intervention plan, directly addressing Core Question 6: identifying the most important factors associated with low review scores and distinguishing primary drivers from secondary contributors . This phase synthesizes the insights from previous sections—the delivery-satisfaction nexus, geographic patterns, and category dynamics—into a cohesive framework for decision-making. The objective is to move beyond identifying correlations and provide Olist's leadership with a clear, evidence-backed roadmap for improving customer satisfaction in the most impactful areas. This is achieved through a hybrid analytical approach combining multivariate statistics with granular segmentation, followed by a systematic prioritization of interventions based on quantified business exposure.

The first layer of the root cause analysis employs a multivariate logistic regression model. The dependent variable is defined as a binary outcome: `low_review`, which is assigned a value of 1 if the `review_score` is 2 or less (representing a strongly negative review), and 0 otherwise [[82](https://medium.com/@emmmazyh/customer-satisfaction-rate-improvement-for-olist-store-ab33d00613d1)]. This framing focuses the model on capturing the strongest signals of customer dissatisfaction. The independent variables are selected based on the preceding exploratory analyses and include a mix of continuous and categorical predictors. Key predictors in the model specification will be:
*   **Delivery Performance:** The `delay_days` variable, treated as a categorical variable with ordered levels (e.g., Early, On-Time, 1-3 Days Late, etc.), to capture the non-linear impact of delay severity.
*   **Logistics Durations:** `delivery_duration` (total time from purchase to delivery) and `distance` (physical distance traveled).
*   **Economic Factors:** `freight_ratio` (freight cost relative to product price) and `order_value`.
*   **Product and Transactional Context:** Dummy variables for `product_category_name` and `payment_type`.
*   **Geographic Context:** Dummy variables for `customer_state` and `seller_state`.

The application of logistic regression is justified as it allows for the estimation of the probability of a categorical outcome (low review) based on multiple predictor variables [[221](https://uc-r.github.io/logistic_regression)]. The primary outputs of interest are the coefficients (or their exponentiated form, odds ratios) and their corresponding p-values. An odds ratio greater than 1 for a predictor indicates that an increase in that variable is associated with higher odds of a low review score, while an odds ratio less than 1 indicates the opposite. By ranking the predictors based on the magnitude of their odds ratios, a hierarchy of drivers emerges. For example, the analysis might reveal that the "Severe Delay" bucket has the highest odds ratio, confirming it as the primary driver. Following that, the model might identify "High Freight Ratio" and "Customer in Northern Region" as the next most significant predictors. This process provides a statistically-informed ranking of factors, directly addressing the need to distinguish primary from secondary contributors . It is critical to acknowledge the limitations: this is an observational study, and the results show association, not causation [[36](https://andrematiello.com/projects/ba-04-ab-test.html)]. However, by controlling for numerous other variables, the model isolates the unique contribution of each factor, offering a much stronger basis for prioritization than univariate correlations.

The second layer of the root cause analysis moves from statistical abstraction to practical diagnosis through segmentation. The goal is to identify the specific, high-risk segments where the drivers identified in the regression model converge to produce the worst customer outcomes. This involves creating cross-tabulations and heatmaps that overlay the most significant predictors. For example, a pivot table could be constructed with `Severe Delay` (Yes/No) as rows and `Customer State` (North, South, etc.) as columns, showing the count and percentage of 1-star reviews in each cell. This immediately highlights which geographic areas are most vulnerable to the impact of severe delays. This analysis can be extended to three or four dimensions, for instance, by adding `Product Category` to the analysis.

The output of this segmentation is a set of high-risk operational niches. For example, the analysis might uncover that "Orders containing 'Heavy Furniture' shipped from 'São Paulo' to customers in 'Remote Northern States', and experiencing a delay of more than 7 days, constitute only 2% of all orders but account for a staggering 20% of all 1-star reviews." This type of finding is immensely powerful because it provides a precise, measurable target for intervention. It tells Olist not just *what* the problem is (severe delays), but *where* it is happening (specific seller-region-category combinations) and *how much* of the problem it represents (a disproportionate share of low reviews). This is the essence of translating data into an operational playbook. The identification of these non-obvious segments and the discovery of hidden concentrations of risk are key differentiators that demonstrate deep analytical insight .

Once these high-risk segments are identified, the final step is to quantify their business exposure and prioritize interventions accordingly. This involves calculating the share of key metrics for each segment. For each identified high-risk cluster, the following metrics should be calculated:
*   **Order Exposure:** The percentage of total delivered orders that fall into this segment.
*   **Revenue Exposure:** The percentage of total platform revenue (from delivered orders) generated by this segment.
*   **Dissatisfaction Exposure:** The percentage of total low reviews (≤2 stars) originating from this segment.

This creates a "risk profile" for each segment. A segment with high revenue exposure and high dissatisfaction exposure is a P0 priority. An intervention here would protect both customer goodwill and significant revenue streams. A segment with low revenue but extremely high dissatisfaction (e.g., a niche category with angry, vocal customers) might also warrant attention to prevent brand damage. Based on this assessment, interventions can be categorized into a prioritization framework:

| Priority Level | Description | Example Intervention |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **P0** | Must Fix. High exposure to both revenue and dissatisfaction. Highest risk to the business. | Partner with top revenue-generating sellers in the North region to audit and optimize their fulfillment and logistics processes for heavy goods. |
| **P1** | High Priority. Significant dissatisfaction exposure, even if revenue exposure is moderate. Addresses major pain points. | Implement a geographically-tailored communication strategy for long-haul shipments to manage customer expectations and reduce frustration. |
| **P2** | Longer-term. Low exposure but indicates a systemic issue that could grow. Good for future planning. | Conduct a feasibility study on establishing a regional fulfillment center in the Center-West to reduce transit times for that corridor. |

This prioritization framework provides a clear, data-driven rationale for where to allocate Olist's limited operational resources. The recommendations are specific, tied to evidence from the segmentation analysis, and framed with expected business impact. For example, the P0 recommendation is not a vague call to "improve logistics in the North"; it is a targeted directive to engage with a specific group of sellers whose business model is demonstrably at risk. This level of specificity and actionability is the ultimate goal of the analysis and is what will make the submission stand out .

In conclusion, the final phase of this research delivers a powerful, integrated solution. By first using multivariate statistics to rank the drivers of customer dissatisfaction and then using segmentation to pinpoint the exact operational contexts where these drivers converge, the analysis identifies the most critical failure points in the Olist ecosystem. The subsequent quantification of business exposure and the development of a prioritized intervention plan transform these insights into a strategic action plan. This approach provides Olist's leadership with a clear, evidence-based pathway to enhance customer satisfaction and support sustainable growth, fulfilling the core objectives of the hackathon challenge.
